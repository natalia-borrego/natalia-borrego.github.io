Research

Ecological context, social strategies, and behavioral flexibility.

African lions are my primary comparative system because their social architecture persists across populations that experience very different ecological conditions. I use those contrasts, together with controlled experiments, to ask when social strategies change and what mechanisms make those adjustments possible.

Research in progress

A few questions currently driving my work.

These projects represent several active strands of a broader research program. Some are analyses I am leading within shared field infrastructure; others are programme-level questions pursued collaboratively.

Cooperation · resource acquisition

Current analysis led by me

Why do lions hunt alone?

Lions are iconic cooperative hunters, yet our Kalahari data show that most prey are captured by individuals—even large and potentially dangerous prey. I am testing when ecological conditions make immediate individual action more advantageous than waiting for partners, and how the benefits of a kill are distributed afterward.

Communication · risk

Comparative analysis and manuscript led by me

When does it pay to stop roaring?

Roaring is a conspicuous territorial signal: it can advertise ownership to rivals, but it also reveals a lion's location. I am testing whether roaring rates decline—or disappear—when the benefits of territorial advertisement are reduced and the costs of being detectable increase in landscapes where lions face high human-wildlife conflict.

Social organization · ecological variation

Collaborative programme question

How flexible are lion societies?

Lions live in fission–fusion societies in which group composition, spacing, and association can change over hours, days, and seasons. Within the comparative programme I co-lead, we ask how ecological conditions alter that flexibility: when individuals remain together, when they separate, how often they reunite, and whether the same social architecture is maintained across populations that differ strongly in resources, density, and risk.

African lions as a comparative system

One species across very different environments.

Lions retain the same basic social architecture across environments, but their opportunities to associate, communicate, cooperate, and compete change dramatically. Comparing populations across ecological gradients allows us to separate what is stable about lion sociality from what changes with local conditions.

Namibia

Low density · mountainous · high shared-landscape risk

Lions occupy large ranges in a resource-limited semi-arid landscape where people and livestock occur throughout the system.

Botswana

Low density · flat pan system · Kalahari ecology

Long-term field collaboration combines direct observation, biologging, and San tracking expertise in a sparse, semi-arid system near the human-use boundary.

Zimbabwe

Resource-rich · high density · contrasting social opportunity

This population provides a productive, high-density contrast in which study lions are spatially removed from the immediate human-use boundary.

Research approach

From experiments to whole-group biologging.

I use the method needed to resolve the biological uncertainty: controlled experiments when competing mechanisms must be separated, direct observation and tracking when behavior must be reconstructed on the ground, and biologging and acoustics when interactions unfold beyond human observation.

Field ecology & sensing

Recovering behavior that is difficult to see.

Whole-group biologging allows social interactions to be reconstructed across space and time, while direct observation and tracking provide the behavioral and ecological ground truth needed to interpret sensor data.

Experimental & ex-situ research

Testing mechanisms that field patterns alone cannot resolve.

Controlled studies complement my field research by allowing me to isolate the cognitive and social processes that can produce patterns observed in the wild. My experimental work has examined innovation, learning and memory, cooperative problem solving, social tolerance, and behavioral flexibility in large carnivores.

I have conducted non-invasive behavioral research with captive lions and other carnivores through collaborations with sanctuaries and rehabilitation facilities in South Africa and AZA-accredited zoological institutions across the United States. These settings provide repeated access to known individuals and controlled social contexts, making it possible to test hypotheses that cannot be manipulated experimentally in free-ranging populations.

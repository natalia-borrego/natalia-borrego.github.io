Research

Ecological context, social strategies, and behavioral flexibility.

African lions are my primary comparative system because their social architecture persists across populations that experience very different ecological conditions. I use those contrasts, together with controlled experiments, to ask when social strategies change and what mechanisms make those adjustments possible.

Research questions

Questions that organize my research.

My research asks how ecological conditions change the social strategies available to animals, the costs and benefits of those strategies, and the flexibility animals have to adjust when conditions change. I approach these problems at multiple levels, from individual decisions to the organization of animal societies.

Social strategies · costs & benefits

When does social behavior pay?

Social behavior can provide access to partners, information, resources, and collective defense, but acting together also carries costs. I ask how ecological conditions alter the balance between acting alone and acting socially, and whether the individuals that bear the costs of collective action are the same individuals that receive its benefits.

One current test: in the Kalahari, why do lions often capture prey alone, and how are the benefits of those kills distributed afterward?

Behavioral flexibility · ecological risk

When should behavior change?

Animals repeatedly encounter environments in which the value of a familiar behavior changes. I ask when ecological conditions favor maintaining an established strategy and when they favor reducing, abandoning, or reorganizing it.

One current collaborative test: in an analysis I co-lead, we ask whether territorial roaring declines—or disappears— when the benefits of advertisement are reduced and revealing location becomes costly.

Social organization · ecological variation

How flexible are animal societies?

Social systems are not static. Within the comparative lion programme I co-lead, we ask how ecological conditions alter association, spacing, fission–fusion dynamics, and other features of social organization across populations that differ strongly in resources, density, and risk.

African lions make this question tractable because the same basic social architecture persists across populations while opportunities to associate, communicate, cooperate, and compete vary dramatically.

Where I work

Field and ex-situ systems across southern Africa and the United States.

My research depends on long-term relationships with field teams, scientific partners, and zoological institutions. Together, these systems give me access to lion populations living under very different ecological conditions and to controlled settings where behavioral mechanisms can be tested directly.

Botswana

Kalahari field ecology & long-term tracking collaboration

I work with Leopard Ecology & Conservation and San tracking collaborators in a low-density, semi-arid lion system. Long-term field knowledge, spoor reconstruction, direct observation, and biologging make it possible to study cooperation and social behavior where lions are difficult to observe directly.

Namibia

Desert-adapted lions in a shared human-wildlife landscape

Through the Ombonde Research Team, I work in a mountainous, resource-limited landscape where lions occur at low density and share space extensively with people and livestock. This system provides a powerful context for studying behavioral flexibility, risk, movement, and communication.

Zimbabwe

A high-density, resource-rich ecological contrast

I work with a long-term conservancy partner in a productive lion population where social opportunities and competitive pressures differ markedly from the semi-arid systems. This contrast helps distinguish ecological responses from features of lion social behavior that remain stable across environments.

South Africa & United States

Controlled behavioral research with known individuals

I conduct non-invasive cognition and behavior research through collaborations with sanctuaries and rehabilitation facilities in South Africa and AZA-accredited zoological institutions throughout the United States. These settings allow repeated experimental tests of cognition, learning, social tolerance, and cooperative problem solving.

In South Africa, these ex-situ collaborations sit alongside long-term professional relationships with field researchers and regional lion networks, including the Lion Information Management Forum (LIMF).

Research approach

From experiments to whole-group biologging.

I use the method needed to resolve the biological uncertainty: controlled experiments when competing mechanisms must be separated, direct observation and tracking when behavior must be reconstructed on the ground, and biologging and acoustics when interactions unfold beyond human observation.

Field ecology & sensing

Recovering behavior that is difficult to see.

Whole-group biologging allows social interactions to be reconstructed across space and time, while direct observation and tracking provide the behavioral and ecological ground truth needed to interpret sensor data.

Experimental & ex-situ research

Testing mechanisms that field patterns alone cannot resolve.

Controlled studies complement my field research by allowing me to isolate the cognitive and social processes that can produce patterns observed in the wild. My experimental work has examined innovation, learning and memory, cooperative problem solving, social tolerance, and behavioral flexibility in large carnivores.

I have conducted non-invasive behavioral research with captive lions and other carnivores through collaborations with sanctuaries and rehabilitation facilities in South Africa and AZA-accredited zoological institutions across the United States. These settings provide repeated access to known individuals and controlled social contexts, making it possible to test hypotheses that cannot be manipulated experimentally in free-ranging populations.

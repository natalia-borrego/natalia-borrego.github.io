How do ecological conditions shape social behavior?

I study how animals respond to social and ecological challenges, from cognition, learning, and cooperative problem solving to collective behavior and sensory ecology. I combine experiments, field observation, tracking, acoustics, and biologging to understand both why social strategies vary and the mechanisms that produce them.

Research trajectory

From individual minds to animal societies.

My research began with a question about cognition: how do the social and ecological challenges animals face shape the way they solve problems? Experimental studies of carnivores led me from innovation, learning, and memory to cooperative problem solving and behavioral flexibility. Those questions now extend into the wild, where I ask how individual decisions scale into social organization and collective behavior under different ecological conditions.

01 · Cognition & innovation

How do animals solve new problems?

My experimental work examines innovation, learning, memory, and behavioral flexibility, asking how ecological and social conditions shape the cognitive challenges animals face.

02 · Cooperation & social tolerance

What allows individuals to solve problems together?

Studies of cooperative problem solving led me to ask how social tolerance, partner availability, and the costs and benefits of acting together shape collective outcomes.

03 · Societies in changing environments

How does ecology reorganize social behavior?

I now extend these questions across wild populations, testing how ecological conditions alter association, communication, cooperation, information use, and other social strategies.

The common thread is behavioral flexibility: what changes when animals face different social partners, resources, risks, and information—and what mechanisms make those adjustments possible?

Current projects

Questions I am working on now.

My current work uses ecological contrasts to identify where familiar assumptions about lion behavior begin to break down—and then asks what those differences reveal about the conditions that favor particular behavioral strategies.

Cooperation · resource acquisition

Current analysis led by me

Why do lions hunt alone?

Lions are iconic cooperative hunters, yet our Kalahari data show that most prey are captured by individuals—even large and potentially dangerous prey. I am testing when ecological conditions make immediate individual action more advantageous than waiting for partners, and how the benefits of a kill are distributed afterward.

Communication · risk

Comparative analysis and manuscript led by me

When does it pay to stop roaring?

Roaring is a conspicuous territorial signal: it can advertise ownership to rivals, but it also reveals a lion's location. I am testing whether roaring rates decline—or disappear—when the benefits of territorial advertisement are reduced and the costs of being detectable increase in landscapes where lions face high human-wildlife conflict.

Social organization · ecological variation

Collaborative programme question

How flexible are lion societies?

Lions live in fission–fusion societies in which group composition, spacing, and association can change over hours, days, and seasons. Within the comparative programme I co-lead, we ask how ecological conditions alter that flexibility: when individuals remain together, when they separate, how often they reunite, and whether the same social architecture is maintained across populations that differ strongly in resources, density, and risk.

Research approach

Across ecological gradients, from field observation to experiment.

I use complementary study systems and methods to separate ecological pattern from behavioral mechanism. Comparative field research reveals how social behavior changes across environments; tracking and biologging recover interactions that are difficult to observe directly; controlled ex-situ studies allow me to test mechanisms that cannot readily be isolated in the wild.

Comparative field ecology

The same social system under different ecological conditions.

My field research spans lion populations that differ in resource availability, population density, landscape structure, and exposure to people. These ecological contrasts provide natural tests of when social strategies remain stable and when they reorganize.

Experimental & ex-situ research

Testing mechanisms that field patterns alone cannot resolve.

Controlled studies complement my field research by allowing me to isolate the cognitive and social processes that can produce patterns observed in the wild. My experimental work has examined innovation, learning and memory, cooperative problem solving, social tolerance, and behavioral flexibility in large carnivores.

I have conducted non-invasive behavioral research with captive lions and other carnivores through collaborations with sanctuaries and rehabilitation facilities in South Africa and AZA-accredited zoological institutions across the United States. These settings provide repeated access to known individuals and controlled social contexts, making it possible to test hypotheses that cannot be manipulated experimentally in free-ranging populations.
